My collaboration with [Christopher A. Del Negro](https://people.wm.edu/~cadeln/) at William & Mary brings mathematical biology and cellular biophysics together with experimental neuroscience to understand the neural control of breathing. We focus on the preBötzinger complex (preBötC), a brainstem network with a central role in inspiratory rhythm generation. Our work asks how this network produces ordinary breaths and sighs, and how opioid signalling alters the neurons that sustain respiratory activity.

### Two rhythms in one network

Normal breathing and sighing operate on markedly different timescales. Both rhythms can be studied in neonatal-mouse brainstem slices that retain the preBötC and inspiratory motor output. These reduced preparations allow us to investigate cellular and synaptic mechanisms alongside mathematical models.

Our 2020 study showed that sigh timing is more variable than commonly assumed: a sigh need not follow an ordinary inspiratory event. Blocking glycinergic and GABAergic inhibition did not abolish coupling between the rhythms or the pause after a sigh. The findings supported excitatory coupling and a refractory origin for the post-sigh pause. [1]

In 2024, we combined modelling with experiments to explain how a shared neuronal population can generate both rhythms. In the model, recurrent excitation, synaptic depression and adaptation produce the faster inspiratory rhythm, while slower intracellular calcium dynamics generate sighs. Experiments in neonatal-mouse slices supported key predictions: partially reducing calcium uptake into the endoplasmic reticulum accelerated sighs, whereas reducing calcium release slowed or stopped them. The model provides a mechanistic account of how sigh frequency can change separately from ordinary inspiration without requiring separate dedicated neuronal populations. Del Negro and I contributed equally to this study. [2]

### Opioid signalling and respiratory neurons

Our 2026 study with Eva Kalajian and Marco Stettler examined how μ-opioid receptor activation changes preBötC excitability. The receptor gene, Oprm1, was expressed in substantial fractions of both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, but the agonist DAMGO reduced excitability selectively in the glutamatergic neurons studied. Whole-cell and single-channel recordings showed enhanced Kir3, or GIRK, potassium-channel activity through a membrane-delimited signalling mechanism consistent with Gβγ action. [3]

This identifies a postsynaptic route by which opioids can weaken excitatory respiratory drive. It also suggests downstream signalling targets for future investigations of opioid-induced respiratory depression. Whether such targets can support effective interventions remains an open research question.

### Molecular context

Related transcriptomic collaborations characterize the molecular identities of preBötC neurons, including electrophysiologically recorded Dbx1-derived cells and single-cell profiles of inspiratory neurons. These datasets complement physiological experiments by documenting the genes expressed in the populations being studied. [4, 5]
